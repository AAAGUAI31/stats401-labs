STATS 401 · INDIVIDUAL PROJECT

Visualization Critique and Redesign

Replacing decorative radial comparison with aligned visual encodings—while keeping country performance separate from personal priorities.

01 · ORIGINAL VISUALIZATION

OECD Better Life Index

The Better Life Index invites members of the public to compare 38 OECD countries across 11 dimensions of current well-being. Its flower glyphs turn each country into a memorable profile: petal length represents performance, petal width represents the importance assigned by the viewer, and flower height represents a preference-weighted overall score.

The intended tasks are exploratory—express personal priorities, see how rankings respond, and notice broad differences among countries. The original is engaging, but its radial geometry makes precise country-to-country comparisons difficult.

Critique and redesign rationale

Original visualization and context

The OECD Better Life Index is an interactive public-facing visualization of well-being across 38 OECD countries. Its current version summarizes 11 dimensions—housing, income and wealth, work and job quality, social connections, knowledge and skills, environmental quality, civic engagement, health, subjective well-being, safety, and work-life balance—on normalized scales from 0 to 10. The intended message is that national well-being is multidimensional and that the “best” country depends partly on what an individual values. Its audience is a broad public rather than statistical specialists. Viewers should be able to express priorities, compare countries, examine multidimensional profiles, and see how different priorities change a composite ranking.

Strengths and weaknesses

The original has two important strengths. First, the flower metaphor is memorable and inviting. Eleven colored petals make the abstract idea of multidimensional well-being feel personal, and the unusual form can encourage exploration. Second, the priority sliders provide meaningful interaction. They show that a composite index is constructed rather than inevitable, and the animated reordering gives immediate feedback when a viewer changes a value.

However, the design is weak for comparison. Petals radiate in different directions and have no shared baseline, so comparing Finland and Denmark on health requires judging lengths at separate angles. Position along a common scale would support more accurate comparison. A second problem is that each petal carries two quantities: length encodes country performance while angular width responds to user priority. A large petal can therefore mean a strong outcome, a high priority, or both. The display blends evidence about the country with a preference supplied by the user.

Third, ranking is unnecessarily difficult. To answer “Which country performs best on work-life balance?” a viewer must find the same colored petal in every flower and compare dozens of radial lengths. Even when countries are sorted by an overall score, the relevant dimension is not aligned. Fourth, the chart becomes visually crowded as the number of countries grows. Thirty-eight glyphs, each with 11 colored petals, create hundreds of marks. Country labels, stems, colors, shapes, and vertical position compete for attention, increasing search time and making close values hard to distinguish.

Redesign rationale

The redesign makes four connected changes. First, it replaces flowers with a heatmap in which rows are countries and columns are well-being dimensions. Every score occupies an aligned rectangular grid and uses the same fixed 0–10 color scale. A viewer can scan across a row for a country profile or down a column for international comparison. Exact values remain available on demand without filling every cell with text.

Second, it separates performance from preference. Heatmap colors always encode OECD performance scores. Priority sliders feed only a clearly labeled weighted-overall calculation, so changing a preference cannot make the underlying evidence appear better or worse. Third, the sort control supports all 11 dimensions plus the weighted overall score. Reordering countries from high to low converts a slow visual search into a direct ranking task, while highlighting the active column preserves context. Finally, selecting a country opens a horizontal dot plot. All 11 dots share a zero-to-ten axis, making within-country strengths and weaknesses more precise than radial petals.

Original versus redesign

The new view makes several tasks easier: finding the highest or lowest country for one dimension, comparing two countries on the same measure, spotting broad cross-country patterns, and inspecting one country on a common scale. It also makes the role of personal priorities explicit and limited to the composite score. The redesign does sacrifice some of the original’s playfulness and emotional appeal. Heatmap color is less precise than position, so exact values still require the tooltip or detail plot. In addition, normalized scores hide the original units and depend on the countries and indicators included in this 2026 version. OECD notes that versions of the Better Life Index are not directly comparable when indicators and calculation assumptions change. The redesign therefore improves comparison within this snapshot, not historical comparison across versions.
